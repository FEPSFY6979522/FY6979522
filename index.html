Evaluating three-day moving-average signals for quantitative trading decisions

Abstract

A moving-average rule gives a clear answer about when to hold an asset, but that answer may not lead to a good trade. This paper examines a three-day rule using eight illustrative prices and compares historical research on trading signals with research on weaknesses in testing. The example shows how signals are calculated; it does not measure investment returns. Published findings give reasons to investigate moving-average strategies, but do not establish that this particular rule works today. Its usefulness depends on when trades occur, their costs and how the rule is tested.

Introduction

Quantitative trading uses numerical data and defined rules to guide decisions. These rules can be tested against historical prices, although good results can sometimes be misleading (Bailey et al., 2017). The important question is not just how a rule works, but whether there is a sound reason to act on it.

A simple moving average (SMA) is the average price over a chosen number of periods. As a new price becomes available, the oldest price leaves the calculation. Moving averages smooth price data, but this smoothing introduces a delay relative to the original prices (Fidelity, no date).

This paper asks how far three-day moving-average signals can support trading decisions. Eight invented prices make the calculation easy to follow, while published research helps assess its usefulness. Three days is a convenient window for this explanation, rather than a choice shown to give the best results.

Analysis and discussion

The calculation

For a three-day SMA, add the latest three closing prices and divide by three. Day 3 gives (£100 + £102 + £104) ÷ 3 = £102. Day 4 drops the oldest price, giving (£102 + £104 + £103) ÷ 3 = £103. The first two days lack enough observations.

A simple decision rule

The rule says to hold the asset when its closing price is above the SMA, and stay out when it is equal to or below it. “Hold” means buy if not already invested, or remain invested. “Stay out” means sell if invested, or remain out. The example does not use borrowed money or positions that profit from falling prices.

The signal becomes available after the close, so any action occurs later, potentially at a different price. Testing a trade as though future information were already known introduces look-ahead bias (CFA Institute, 2026). The table shows signals, not completed trades or returns.

What the example shows

Day 3 gives a hold signal, while day 4 gives a stay-out signal because the price equals the average. Day 7 gives another hold signal. Anyone applying the same rule gets the same answers. However, changes between holding and staying out could require several trades, and the rule cannot tell us what the next price will be.

Historical evidence and its limits

There is research suggesting that moving-average signals can contain useful information. A study of the Dow Jones index from 1897 to 1986 found higher returns after buy signals than after sell signals (Brock et al., 1992). That gives a reason to study these rules, but it does not prove that this three-day example is effective. The researchers tested different strategies in a different period. The reported difference in returns also does not guarantee that a trader could make a profit after costs.

Limitations

A moving average uses past prices and cannot predict unexpected news. Short windows react quickly, but may also respond to small price fluctuations. Longer windows smooth more of the variation but respond more slowly (Fidelity, no date). Neither choice guarantees that the next price movement will be favourable.

Transaction fees reduce the amount left in a portfolio (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2014). Buying and selling prices can also differ, as can expected and actual execution prices. Frequent signal changes therefore matter: a small apparent advantage may not survive the costs of acting on it.

Testing on historical data is called backtesting. It approximates investing but cannot assume that past conditions represent the future (CFA Institute, 2026). Research on backtest overfitting shows how selecting the best-looking historical rule can fit chance patterns rather than a dependable relationship (Bailey et al., 2017). Keeping data aside is not sufficient if it influences later choices or many trials are ignored.

The two studies answer different questions. Brock and colleagues ask whether signals were linked to later returns; Bailey and colleagues ask whether choosing a rule from past results can mislead us. Their findings are therefore not a simple disagreement. To evaluate this rule further, it would need to be compared with simply holding the asset, using realistic costs and periods not used to choose the rule.

There is also a practical issue if trades are automated. Software testing, risk controls and ongoing monitoring are needed (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, 2015). A rule supported by research can still be implemented badly.

Conclusion

A three-day moving average gives a consistent rule that is easy to check. Research gives reasons to investigate such signals, but it does not show that this example would make money today. The rule is useful for organising a decision, not sufficient for making one. Acting on it would need support from tests that account for timing, costs and the risks of choosing a rule that only looks good on past data.
