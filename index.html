Abstract

This paper introduces quantitative trading through a simple numerical rule. A three-day moving average is calculated from invented closing prices and compared with each day's price. The example shows how a rule can produce consistent signals, but also why a signal is not a guarantee of profit. Trading costs, delayed information and changing market conditions must be considered before a strategy could be evaluated using real data.

Introduction

Quantitative trading uses numerical data and defined rules to guide trading decisions. Instead of deciding only from a personal impression, a trader can specify a calculation and apply the same decision rule repeatedly. This makes the method easier to explain and check, although the choice of rule still involves judgement.

A simple moving average (SMA) is the average price over a chosen number of periods. As a new price becomes available, the oldest price leaves the calculation. Fidelity describes moving averages as a way to smooth price data, while noting that smoothing introduces a delay relative to the original prices [1].

The question explored here is: how can a simple numerical rule guide trading decisions, and what are its limitations? A three-day window keeps the arithmetic easy to follow. It is chosen for this explanation, not because it is proven to be suitable for real trading.

Analysis and discussion

The calculation

For a three-day SMA, add the latest three closing prices and divide the total by three. On day 3, the invented prices are £100, £102 and £104. Their average is (£100 + £102 + £104) ÷ 3 = £102. On day 4, the first price is dropped, giving (£102 + £104 + £103) ÷ 3 = £103. No three-day average is available on days 1 and 2.

A simple decision rule

When the closing price is greater than its SMA, the rule signals a target of holding the asset. When the closing price is equal to or below the SMA, it signals staying out. “Hold” means buy if not already invested, or remain invested; “stay out” means sell if invested, or remain out. This example does not use borrowed money or betting on falling prices.

The signal is only known after that day's closing price is available. Any action would therefore occur later, at a price which could be different. Using information before it was available would introduce look-ahead bias, one of the backtesting problems highlighted by CFA Institute [2]. The table describes signals, not completed trades, and does not calculate returns.

What the example shows

Day 3 produces a hold signal, but day 4 produces a stay-out signal because its price equals the average. Day 7 produces another hold signal. The calculation gives a consistent answer, yet frequent changes of signal could lead to repeated trading. A numerical rule removes some uncertainty about how to decide; it does not remove uncertainty about what happens next.

Limitations

A moving average uses past prices and cannot predict unexpected news. Short windows react quickly, but may also respond to small price fluctuations. Longer windows smooth more of the variation but respond more slowly [1]. Neither choice guarantees that the next price movement will be favourable.

Real transactions can involve fees. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission explains that transaction fees are charged when investments are bought, sold or exchanged, reducing the amount left in a portfolio [3]. There can also be a gap between buying and selling prices, and execution may happen at a different price from the one expected. These effects can reduce any gains.

The eight invented prices below demonstrate arithmetic only: they are not evidence that the strategy works. A meaningful evaluation would require real data, realistic trading assumptions and separate data not used to choose the rule. Testing a strategy using historical data is called backtesting. CFA Institute explains that this aims to approximate real investing, while warning that historical conditions may not represent the future [2].

If a rule is later automated to place trades, checking its calculation is not enough. FINRA's guidance for U.S. trading firms identifies software testing, risk controls and continued monitoring as important safeguards [4]. This paper does not implement automated order placement, but the guidance shows why a simple signal should not be treated as a complete trading system.

Conclusion

A moving-average rule is a straightforward introduction to quantitative trading because its inputs, calculation and decisions can be inspected. This example illustrates how signals are generated, rather than whether they make money. A consistent rule can still produce losses, so costs, risk and careful testing matter as much as the calculation itself.
